Whole Life vs Term Life Insurance in Virginia

Term or whole life? Here's how the two compare on cost, length of coverage and cash value, when each one tends to fit, and which Virginia consumer protections apply either way. Every outside rule links to its source.

By Analiza Soratorio, life insurance agent licensed in Virginia · Updated November 15, 2026

The short answer

Term life insurance covers you for a set number of years and generally costs the least for the amount of coverage at the start. Whole life insurance is designed to last for your whole life, builds cash value and generally costs more at the start. A common approach is to use term for needs that end (a mortgage, the years children are at home) and look at whole life for needs that don't. Which one fits depends on how long you need coverage, what you can comfortably pay and what else you want the policy to do.

What term life insurance is

The Virginia Bureau of Insurance describes term insurance as protection for a set period of time, called the policy term. If you die during the term, your beneficiaries receive the death benefit. If you outlive the term, the policy expires with no value and pays nothing (Virginia Consumer's Guide for Life Insurance). The same guide notes that term generally offers the most pure insurance protection for the lowest premium.

Term policies come in a few shapes:

  • Level term keeps the same death benefit for the whole term.
  • Decreasing term has a death benefit that shrinks over time, and is often used to match a mortgage balance as it goes down.
  • Increasing term starts at one amount and rises at set intervals.

Two features matter a lot when you compare term policies. A renewable term policy lets you continue coverage when the term ends, even if your health has changed, but the premium is usually recalculated at your older age each time you renew. The Bureau warns that renewal premiums can become very expensive with age and that some term policies cannot be renewed past age 65 or 70 (Virginia Bureau of Insurance). A convertible term policy lets you trade it for a permanent policy before the term ends, even if you are no longer in good health, though the new permanent policy will cost more than the term did.

The NAIC adds a simple rule of thumb: term is meant to provide lower-cost coverage for a specific period, and most term policies don't build cash value you can use later (NAIC Life Insurance Buyer's Guide).

What whole life insurance is

Whole life is a kind of permanent insurance that stays in force for your whole life as long as the premiums are paid. According to the Bureau, the premium is based on your age when you buy, generally does not go up, and is lower the younger you are when you buy. It may be much higher at first than the premium for the same amount of term insurance, but lower than what you would pay to keep renewing term into your later years (Virginia Consumer's Guide for Life Insurance).

Whole life also builds cash value: an amount that builds over the life of the policy, as set out in the contract, and that the insurer would pay you, often after a surrender charge, if you cancelled the policy. You can also borrow against it. Cash value has enough moving parts that it has its own guide: How Whole Life Cash Value Works.

Whole life has variations too. With limited-pay whole life, such as a 20-pay policy, premiums stop after a set number of years and coverage continues for life; each premium is higher because you pay for fewer years. The NAIC notes that whole life premiums are typically paid on a set schedule, which is one of the main differences from universal life, where payments can flex (NAIC Life Insurance Buyer's Guide).

Why whole life costs more at the start

With term, the insurer pays only if death happens during the term. With whole life, coverage is designed to last until death, and the policy also builds cash value. The NAIC explains that cash value premiums are higher at the beginning than term premiums for the same coverage, and that the part of the premium not used for the cost of insurance builds up the policy's cash value (NAIC Life Insurance Buyer's Guide, 1997/2007 edition).

That is also why the comparison changes with time. If you only need coverage for 15 or 20 years, term is usually the lower-cost way to get it. If you want coverage for your whole life, the NAIC says cash value insurance may be more cost-effective (NAIC Life Insurance Buyer's Guide). Neither type is "better" in general; they solve different problems.

When term life tends to fit

The Bureau says term works well when your need for protection is temporary, or when you need a large amount of coverage but can't afford a large premium. A young family is the classic case: you can protect the immediate needs of your spouse and children with term and, if the policy is convertible, move to permanent coverage later when money is less tight (Virginia Consumer's Guide for Life Insurance).

Common term needs include:

  • Paying off a mortgage if you die before it is paid off.
  • Replacing income until the youngest child is grown.
  • Covering a business loan or the years until a partner buy-out is funded.

When whole life tends to fit

Whole life is worth a look when the need does not end on a date. Examples include final expenses, leaving money to children or a charity whenever you die, supporting a family member who will be dependent for life, or helping heirs settle an estate. Some people also value the cash value and the fixed premium schedule. The Bureau's guide reminds buyers that the main purpose of life insurance is financial protection, and that other products exist for goals such as retirement or college savings (Virginia Bureau of Insurance). If cash value is the main reason you are interested, it's worth comparing it with those other options too.

Virginia consumer protections to know

Whichever type you choose, several protections apply to individual life policies sold in Virginia. All of these come from the Virginia Bureau of Insurance consumer guide:

  • 10-day free look. Virginia law requires individual life insurance policies to give you at least 10 days after you receive the policy to examine it. If you return it in that window, you get a refund of the premium paid and the policy is treated as void from the start. Keep a dated receipt when you return it.
  • Grace period. If you miss a premium, the grace period is usually 31 days, and the policy stays in force while you pay without penalty.
  • Contestable period. Except for non-payment of premiums, the insurer usually cannot contest the policy after it has been in force for two years during the insured's lifetime. This is one reason to answer application questions completely and accurately.
  • Licensed companies and agents. The Bureau recommends buying from a company licensed in Virginia, and it can tell you whether a company or agent is licensed in the state.

If you already have a policy

Be careful about replacing a policy you already own. The Bureau points out that dropping a permanent policy for another one may not pay off, because cash value tends to build faster as a policy ages, and a new policy starts a new two-year contestable period. If you are considering a replacement, ask for a written side-by-side comparison and talk to your current agent or insurer first (Virginia Bureau of Insurance). The NAIC adds: don't cancel your current policy until the new one is in force (NAIC Life Insurance Buyer's Guide).

Taxes, briefly

For both term and whole life, the IRS says life insurance proceeds a beneficiary receives because the insured person died generally aren't included in gross income and don't have to be reported. Interest paid on those proceeds is taxable, and different rules can apply if the policy was transferred for value (IRS: Life insurance & disability insurance proceeds). Cash value has its own tax rules, covered in the cash value guide. For your own situation, ask a tax professional.

Questions to ask before you buy

  • How long do I actually need coverage, and for which needs?
  • If this is term, is it renewable and convertible, and until what age?
  • If this is whole life, what part of the premium or policy value is not guaranteed? The NAIC recommends asking exactly this (NAIC).
  • Can I see an illustration showing future values, both guaranteed and non-guaranteed?
  • Can I comfortably keep paying this premium for as long as the policy requires?
  • Is the insurer licensed in Virginia, and is the agent?
