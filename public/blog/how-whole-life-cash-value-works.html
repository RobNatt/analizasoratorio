How Whole Life Cash Value Works

Cash value is the savings side of a whole life policy. Here's where it comes from, why it starts slowly, what's guaranteed and what isn't, and how loans, surrenders and taxes work, with every outside rule linked to its source.

By Analiza Soratorio, life insurance agent licensed in Virginia · Updated November 15, 2026

The short answer

Part of each whole life premium pays for the insurance itself; the rest builds the policy's cash value. Cash value is often low in the first years and builds over time on the schedule set by the policy. While you are living you can borrow against it, use it to keep the policy going, or surrender the policy for it, and each choice reduces or ends the death benefit. Some of the value is guaranteed by the contract (subject to the insurer's claims-paying ability); dividends, if the policy pays them, are not.

Where cash value comes from

The NAIC explains it this way: cash value policies charge higher premiums at the beginning than term insurance would for the same coverage, and the part of the premium not used for the cost of insurance is invested by the company and builds up the cash value (NAIC Life Insurance Buyer's Guide, 1997/2007 edition).

The Virginia Bureau of Insurance defines cash value as the amount the insurer will pay the policyowner if a permanent policy is surrendered or otherwise ended, and notes that it can serve as collateral for a loan from the insurer. For whole life, the Bureau says the cash value increases every year the policy is in force (Virginia Consumer's Guide for Life Insurance).

Two things follow from that. First, cash value is not a separate account you own on the side; it is part of the policy and is tied to it. Second, the insurer, not you, invests the money, so the growth that is guaranteed is whatever the contract states.

Guaranteed and non-guaranteed values

A whole life illustration usually shows two sets of numbers:

  • Guaranteed values are set out in the contract: the guaranteed cash value at each policy year and the guaranteed death benefit, as long as premiums are paid as required. These guarantees are contractual and subject to the claims-paying ability of the issuing insurer.
  • Non-guaranteed values show what could happen if the insurer pays dividends at an assumed scale. They are not promises.

The NAIC notes that non-variable policies, which include whole life, often guarantee minimums for some features, such as interest or cash value, but not for all of them, and it suggests asking which part of the premium or policy value isn't guaranteed (NAIC Life Insurance Buyer's Guide). When you read an illustration, look at the guaranteed column first and treat everything else as a possibility.

Why cash value starts slowly

The NAIC says that in some cash value policies the values are low in the early years and build later on, while in others they build gradually (NAIC Life Insurance Buyer's Guide). Many policies also charge a surrender charge if you cancel, which the Bureau defines as a fee subtracted from the cash value when you surrender (Virginia Bureau of Insurance).

Dividends on participating policies

Some whole life policies are participating, meaning you may share in the insurer's favourable results through dividends. The Bureau explains that dividends come from the company paying fewer claims than expected, spending less than expected or earning more interest than expected, and that whether a dividend is paid, and how much, cannot be guaranteed. Dividends usually start after the policy has been in force for two or three years, and the Bureau describes them as a "return of excess premium" for tax purposes (Virginia Consumer's Guide for Life Insurance).

Participating policies often let you take dividends in cash, use them to reduce premiums, leave them on deposit or use them to buy small amounts of paid-up additional insurance. The options depend on the policy, so check the contract.

Ways to use cash value while you are living

1. Borrow against it (a policy loan)

According to the Bureau, you can use the cash value as collateral and borrow from the insurer at a specified interest rate, without a credit check. If you die with a loan outstanding, the death benefit is reduced by the loan and the interest owed. If the loan plus interest ever grows larger than the cash value, the policy will lapse (Virginia Bureau of Insurance). The NAIC says the same: unpaid loans and interest come out of the benefit at death, or out of the cash value if you surrender (NAIC).

EXAMPLE: hypothetical numbers, for illustration only

EXAMPLE: A policy has a $100,000 death benefit and $20,000 of cash value. The owner borrows $5,000 at an assumed 6% loan interest rate and pays nothing back. After one year the owner owes about $5,300. If the insured died then, the beneficiaries would receive about $94,700 instead of $100,000. Actual loan rates and terms are set by each policy.

2. Keep the policy going if you stop paying

The Bureau explains that, by law, insurers must make nonforfeiture options available if you stop paying premiums: take the cash value in cash, keep a smaller amount of coverage as reduced paid-up insurance with no further premiums, or keep the full amount as extended term insurance for a limited time. If you don't choose within a set period, usually 60 days from the lapse, the insurer applies an automatic option. Some policies also have an automatic premium loan feature that pays a missed premium from the cash value (Virginia Consumer's Guide for Life Insurance).

3. Surrender the policy

Surrendering ends the coverage, and the insurer pays the cash surrender value: the cash value minus any surrender charge and any outstanding loans. The IRS says that if you surrender a life insurance policy for cash, you include in income any proceeds above your cost. In most cases your cost is the total premiums you paid, minus refunded premiums, rebates, dividends and unrepaid loans that weren't included in your income (IRS Publication 525).

EXAMPLE: hypothetical numbers, for illustration only

EXAMPLE: You paid $30,000 in premiums, received no dividends in cash and have no loans. You surrender and receive $34,000. Under the IRS rule above, the $4,000 above your cost would generally be taxable income, and you would receive a Form 1099-R. If you received $27,000 instead, there would be no gain to report. Your tax professional can confirm how this applies to you.

Watch for a modified endowment contract (MEC)

Federal tax law limits how much you can put into a life insurance policy in its early years and still keep the usual tax treatment. Under the 7-pay test, a policy entered into on or after June 21, 1988 becomes a modified endowment contract if the total paid in during the first seven contract years is more than the net level premiums that would pay up the policy in seven annual payments (26 U.S.C. § 7702A).

Once a policy is a MEC, loans and withdrawals are taxed less favourably, and the taxable part of a distribution can face an extra 10% tax unless an exception applies, such as being age 59½ or older, becoming disabled, or receiving substantially equal periodic payments (26 U.S.C. § 72(e)(10) and § 72(v)). This mostly matters if you plan to pay extra into a policy beyond its scheduled premiums, for example through large paid-up additions. Ask for MEC limits to be shown on any illustration.

What your beneficiaries receive

On a standard whole life policy, beneficiaries receive the death benefit, not the death benefit plus the cash value. Outstanding loans and interest are subtracted, and on participating policies any paid-up additions and dividends left on deposit are added. The Bureau's definition of policy proceeds lists exactly these adjustments (Virginia Bureau of Insurance). The IRS says death proceeds a beneficiary receives generally aren't taxable income, though interest on them is (IRS).

Is cash value a reason to buy?

It can be one reason, but rarely the only one. The Bureau's guide reminds buyers that the main purpose of life insurance is financial protection and that other products exist for goals such as retirement or college funding (Virginia Consumer's Guide for Life Insurance). If you mostly want savings, compare whole life with those options. If you want lifetime coverage and like a contractual premium schedule with a value you can access, cash value may be a useful feature. For the bigger picture, read Whole Life vs Term Life Insurance in Virginia.

How to read a whole life illustration

  • Find the guaranteed cash value and death benefit columns and read those first.
  • Find the dividend or interest assumption behind the non-guaranteed columns, and ask what happens if it is lower.
  • Check the cash surrender value (after surrender charges) in years 1 to 10, not just year 30.
  • Check how loans would affect the death benefit.
  • Ask whether planned extra payments would make the policy a MEC.
  • Ask what part of the premium or policy value isn't guaranteed, as the NAIC recommends (NAIC).
